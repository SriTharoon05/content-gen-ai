# Audio direction

You are directing a voice performance, not configuring a speech synthesiser. The model knows both
*what* to say and *how* to say it, so give it a character, a room and a brief.

## Structure
- **Audio profile** — name and archetype. "Maya R., the late-night documentary narrator."
- **Scene** — where they are and what it feels like. This shapes the read indirectly and is often
  more effective than piling on adjectives.
- **Director's notes** — style, pacing and accent. Specificity wins: "British English as heard in
  Croydon" beats "British accent"; "infectious enthusiasm, the listener should feel part of something"
  beats "energetic". Define only what matters; over-specifying flattens the performance.
- **Sample context** — one line on what this voice normally gets hired for.

## Audio tags
Inline square-bracket modifiers change delivery for the words around them: `[excited]`, `[whispers]`,
`[serious]`, `[curious]`, `[sighs]`, `[gasp]`, `[laughs]`, `[trembling]`, `[sarcastic]`, `[shouting]`,
`[tired]`, `[very fast]`, `[slowly]`, `[one word at a time]`.

- Always write tags in **English**, even when the narration is in another language.
- Tag the hook, the turn and the final line at minimum.
- Roughly one tag every two or three sentences. Tag-spam makes the read jittery.
- Tags are spoken by nobody: they are direction, not content.

## Casting
Pick the voice that already leans the way the story does — a breathy voice for something intimate, a
gravelly one for something old and heavy, an upbeat one for something playful. Fighting the voice
with the notes produces a worse read than choosing a voice that agrees with them.

Use two speakers only when the piece genuinely reads as two people. A monologue with a second voice
bolted on sounds like a mistake.

## Hard rule
Tags and speaker labels change **delivery only**. The spoken words must remain exactly the approved
script, in the same order. This is verified automatically before a single paid call is made, and a
drifted transcript is rejected.
