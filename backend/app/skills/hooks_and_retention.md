# Hooks and retention (short-form)

A scrolling viewer decides in under two seconds. The first beat is not an introduction to the story;
it **is** the reason to stay.

## Openers that fail
- "One day, a man named John…" — a story opener with nothing at stake yet
- "Hey guys, today we're looking at…" — greeting, channel name, throat-clearing
- "Have you ever wondered about the fascinating world of…" — vague curiosity with no specific pull
- Any first line that could sit in front of a hundred different videos

## Openers that work
- **question** — a question the viewer already half-asks themselves.
  "Do you know why your grocery bill keeps climbing even though inflation fell?"
- **claim** — a statement that sounds wrong until you explain it.
  "The safest seat on a plane is the one nobody books."
- **scene** — drop them mid-moment, already in trouble.
  "The lift stopped between floors, and the phone had no signal."
- **number** — a specific, surprising figure.
  "Ninety seconds. That's how long the whole island had."
- **contradiction** — two facts that cannot both be true.
  "The town has no roads in. Two thousand people live there."

## After the hook
- Beat 2 pays the hook forward with new information; it never restates the hook.
- Something must change every few seconds: a new fact, a turn, a complication.
- Name the stakes early — what is lost if this goes wrong.
- The final beat must land the exact promise the hook made. A hook the ending does not pay off is a
  failed video even if every other beat is good.

## Production editorial standard
- Choose a concrete, surprising mechanism, decision or consequence, not a broad topic overview.
- Draft three different openings internally and use the strongest specific one. Vary questions,
  visual puzzles, counterintuitive claims and mid-action scenes; never repeat a catchphrase.
- Establish the object/person and the unanswered question immediately. Explain a first useful
  detail by the second sentence; do not withhold the entire answer until the end.
- Build a causal chain: because this happened, someone did that, which caused this consequence.
  Each new sentence must add an explanation, obstacle or revealing detail, not rephrase the last.
- Use a concrete visual example or comparison to explain the mechanism. Avoid abstract filler
  such as "rewriting the rules", "changing everything", "unlocking possibilities" and empty hype.
- Facts/history/technology must teach a specific accurate takeaway. Separate established facts
  from uncertainty or speculation. Never invent an event, experiment, statistic or historical quote.
- Human stories need an earned practical insight, not a generic motivational closing. Imagined
  scenarios must be identifiable as imagined; do not pass them off as documented real events.
- Read the narration as one flowing paragraph before dividing it into visual beats. An image
  change is not a sentence break or a pause. Keep expressive, natural connected speech.
- In conversations, the second person responds to the actual previous point, challenges or
  clarifies it, and adds something new. Reactions such as "yeah, exactly" fit only when earned.
- Reject a script if its hook promises something the ending never explains, if the middle could
  be removed without losing information, or if it teaches nothing specific worth remembering.
